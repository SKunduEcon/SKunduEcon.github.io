India's Next Growth Decade: From Demand to Productivity

By Shiny Kundu | 16 November 2026

Bottom line. The test of India's next growth phase is whether resilient demand translates into productive investment, higher output per worker and better-paid, more secure work. The semiconductor build-out offers a clear measure of progress. Its wider economic return will depend on reliable power and water, efficient logistics, capable suppliers, technical skills and the quality of India's human capital.
The sectoral call. The most consequential industrial opportunity over the next decade is the integrated electronics and semiconductor manufacturing ecosystem. It spans chip design, fabrication, assembly and testing, components, precision engineering, industrial automation and the power infrastructure that supports production. Its economic importance lies in the capital, technology, skills and supplier capabilities it can draw into India's manufacturing base. Direct employment is likely to remain smaller than in labour-intensive industries; the larger gain could come through productivity spillovers and the development of more sophisticated industrial capabilities.

A strong Q2, and the longer growth question

Today's Q2 coverage points to continued economic momentum. The Times of India's poll of ten economists puts September-quarter real GDP growth at 7.5% or higher, while the Economic Times poll reports a 7.7% median estimate. The ET report also points to average industrial production growth of 7.3% in the quarter and strong revenue and profit growth across the listed non-financial companies in its sample. These are survey estimates, not the official GDP outturn; the NSO is scheduled to publish Q2 GDP on 30 November. The RBI's 7.2% projection and the Finance Ministry's 7.35% nowcast provide useful benchmarks.

The World Bank's October 2026 India Development Update projects 7.1% growth in FY2026-27, supported by domestic demand and exports. Together, the near-term indicators describe a resilient cycle. The longer-run assessment turns on the composition of that growth: whether demand induces investment that raises output per worker, broadens the export base and supports durable real-income gains.

The semiconductor opportunity gives this question a concrete industrial dimension. The EY–India Electronics and Semiconductor Association 2026 report estimates that India's semiconductor market could expand from about US$64 billion in 2026 to US$200 billion by 2035. The figure measures the potential size of the market; it does not estimate domestic value added or employment. India's return will depend on the share of the value chain located domestically, the pace at which projects reach commercial production, and the ability of local suppliers to move into more complex activities.

Fabrication capacity is one part of the investment. The surrounding ecosystem includes materials, equipment, testing, packaging, process engineering, power, water, logistics, research and skilled labour. This network will determine how far semiconductor investment strengthens domestic capabilities and raises productivity in adjacent industries.

What the next five to ten years could look like

India's medium-term growth prospects are best assessed across a range of outcomes. The IMF's 2025 country framework placed potential growth around 6.4–6.5% through 2030-31. The RBI's October 2026 Survey of Professional Forecasters put median real GDP growth at 7.1% for FY2026-27 and 7.0% for FY2027-28. The estimates cover different horizons and use different methodologies. Near-term cyclical momentum can exceed the economy's sustainable medium-term pace.

A reasonable analytical baseline for the coming decade is real growth around 6.5% a year, with an upside path towards 7.5% if investment quality, productivity, labour utilisation and export competitiveness improve together. A downside path around 5.5% becomes more plausible if external shocks persist, private investment disappoints, job creation remains weak or infrastructure and skills bottlenecks prevent capital from being used efficiently. The three paths are scenario assumptions used to illustrate compounding.

Growth, inflation and the interest-rate equilibrium

A decade of strong real growth need not imply a decade of high inflation or persistently high interest rates. The more favourable configuration is one in which supply capacity expands alongside demand. Manufacturing scale, better logistics, reliable electricity, competitive services and higher agricultural productivity can reduce unit costs and ease capacity constraints. In that setting, real incomes can rise without a persistent acceleration in prices.

But the inflation path will remain exposed to food, energy and climate volatility. India's food basket, dependence on imported energy and infrastructure gaps can transmit supply shocks into headline CPI and household expectations. The RBI's inflation-targeting framework, centred on 4% CPI inflation with a tolerance band of 2–6%, provides the nominal anchor. In its October 2026 survey, professional forecasters' ten-year inflation expectations were around 4.2%. That is a reference point, not a guarantee that realised inflation will remain there.

Over five to ten years, the policy rate should be thought of as an outcome of the inflation anchor, the equilibrium real interest rate, fiscal conditions and the risk premium demanded by savers. A stable 4–5% inflation environment would be consistent with a materially lower nominal policy-rate regime than one in which food and energy shocks repeatedly unanchor expectations. Equally, stronger investment demand and a higher equilibrium real rate could keep nominal rates from falling simply because inflation is contained. A precise ten-year repo-rate forecast would imply more certainty than the evidence allows.

For markets, the distinction matters. Higher nominal GDP growth can support corporate revenues, but the valuation of those cash flows also depends on the cost of capital. A durable decline in inflation volatility and sovereign risk premia could lower long-term discount rates. A growth acceleration driven by capital scarcity, energy constraints or fiscal slippage could instead coexist with higher real yields.

The semiconductor test: from investment to productivity

Semiconductors are strategically important because they sit upstream of a wide range of industries: electronics, automobiles, telecoms, industrial equipment, defence, data infrastructure and consumer devices. A domestic ecosystem can create value beyond the wafer or packaged chip itself. Design, fabrication, assembly and testing, equipment maintenance, materials, process engineering, reliability and advanced packaging generate different kinds of technical capability.

Today's Economic Times report on semiconductor hiring cites Boston Consulting Group estimates of more than 100,000 additional jobs over three years, alongside a CIEL HR Services estimate of 67,500 openings over the next 18 months. These are industry projections, not realised employment gains. The composition of hiring is particularly important: demand is expanding beyond chip design into process engineering, equipment maintenance, yield management, reliability engineering and advanced packaging. The report also highlights shortages of experienced specialists. Training can widen the entry-level pipeline; senior process and manufacturing expertise takes longer to develop.

The wider productivity dividend depends on whether local suppliers learn, Indian firms move into more complex functions, technology diffuses to adjacent industries, and domestic production becomes commercially competitive. A semiconductor plant that relies heavily on imported inputs, expertise and continuing subsidies may still have strategic value, but its domestic value added and productivity spillovers will be more limited than those of a dense supplier and innovation ecosystem.

The semiconductor opportunity sits alongside India's broader manufacturing agenda. Electronics assembly, capital goods, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, auto components, renewable-energy equipment and precision engineering all contribute to industrial depth. Each requires predictable regulation, patient capital, access to export markets, reliable infrastructure and firms able to scale.

Infrastructure is productive capacity

Infrastructure's longer-term economic value lies in lowering the cost, uncertainty and time required to move people, goods, energy, data and ideas. Freight corridors, ports, roads and urban transit can enlarge labour and product markets. Reliable electricity and grid capacity are essential for factories and data centres. Water availability, treatment and recycling are particularly important for semiconductor fabrication. Digital infrastructure can lower transaction costs, but it cannot compensate for unreliable physical networks.

Public capital expenditure can crowd in private investment when it resolves network bottlenecks and improves project returns. The next phase therefore requires more than a high capex allocation: project selection, land acquisition, state-level execution, maintenance, urban governance and financial viability determine the return on that spending. The OECD's 2026 assessment identifies infrastructure gaps, including electricity reliability, as constraints on Indian productivity and competitiveness.

There is also a spatial dimension. Industrial clusters can raise productivity through supplier proximity, labour-market depth and knowledge spillovers, but they can widen regional disparities if investment is concentrated in a small number of states. The ability of tier-two and tier-three cities to provide power, water, transport, housing, health and education will influence how widely the gains from industrialisation are distributed.

The binding constraint may be human development

India's demographic scale is an advantage only when people can participate productively in the economy. The World Bank's recent India update notes that employment remains concentrated in low-productivity sectors and that the share of workers in salaried jobs remains limited. The OECD similarly highlights skill shortages and low female labour-force participation as constraints on the country's ability to translate capital accumulation into higher output per worker.

The semiconductor workforce is a useful microcosm. Training more engineers is necessary, but the sector also needs technicians, equipment specialists, production supervisors, quality-control professionals and experienced managers. Industry-linked curricula, apprenticeships, faculty development and access to laboratory infrastructure are as important as headline enrolment. The same principle applies to advanced manufacturing more broadly.

Human development is a direct input into the growth model. Foundational learning shapes the ability of young people to acquire technical skills. Nutrition and public health influence learning outcomes and labour productivity. Affordable urban housing and transport affect access to employment. Women's participation also depends on safe mobility, childcare, workplace design and the availability of formal jobs. The rise in female labour-force participation reported in official PLFS-based releases is encouraging; the next test is the quality, stability and earnings of the work being created.

The IMF has argued that stronger innovation and business dynamism could materially lift India's productivity growth. Its analysis points to large productivity gaps between small and large firms, limited R&D intensity and the need for firms to scale, adopt technology and reallocate resources towards more productive uses. Progress will require competition, predictable rules, stronger research links between universities and firms, and a financing system that backs productive risk-taking.

What to watch: the signposts for 2031 and 2036

The quality of India's growth will become clearer through a small set of observable tests. Does private investment broaden beyond a few large firms and favoured sectors? Does manufacturing's contribution to output and exports rise alongside labour productivity, rather than mainly through low-value assembly? Do real wages and formal employment grow beyond the organised corporate sector? Does inflation volatility decline as food supply chains, energy networks and urban infrastructure improve? Finally, do learning, health and female employment outcomes advance in step with capital expenditure?

These tests are connected. Better human capital raises the return on infrastructure. More reliable infrastructure makes advanced manufacturing viable. Higher productivity supports real wages and corporate earnings. Broader household income growth sustains domestic demand. A stable nominal anchor allows monetary policy to support investment without repeatedly having to suppress inflationary pressure.

The decade's central question: Can India turn the strength of its domestic market into a platform for globally competitive production, and turn that production into more productive, better-paid work? The semiconductor push offers a visible test. The answer will depend on the less visible foundations: firm-level capability, infrastructure reliability, skills, health, education and institutional execution.
