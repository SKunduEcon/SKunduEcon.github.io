India's Next Growth Decade: From Demand to Productivity

By Shiny Kundu | 16 November 2026

Bottom line. India's investment cycle is gathering pace. The harder test is whether capital deepening is accompanied by faster total factor productivity, higher real output per worker and a shift of labour towards more productive firms and activities. The semiconductor programme is one test of industrial capability-building, but economy-wide spillovers remain to be demonstrated. The return on investment will depend on domestic supplier depth, export competitiveness, infrastructure reliability and the quality of skills.
The sectoral call. Semiconductors are a strategically important, capital- and technology-intensive part of India's industrial transition. The ecosystem spans chip design, fabrication, assembly and testing, components, precision engineering, equipment maintenance and the power and water infrastructure required for production. These activities can create channels for supplier learning and technical capability, but the scale of wider spillovers cannot be inferred from announced capacity or subsidy commitments. Direct employment is likely to be smaller than in labour-intensive industries. The economic case therefore rests on measurable domestic value added, competitive yields, local sourcing, exports and diffusion of capabilities into other firms.

Investment is accelerating. Productivity is the test.

The official starting point is stronger than a press poll. MoSPI estimates that real GDP grew by 7.8% in Q1 FY2026-27. Gross fixed capital formation (GFCF) grew by 11.9% year on year, against 5.8% in the corresponding quarter a year earlier. Private final consumption grew by 7.1%, while exports rose by 12.0%. The composition points to a firmer investment and external-demand contribution, although one quarter cannot establish a durable change in the growth model. The NSO's Q2 GDP release is scheduled for 30 November; press-poll estimates should remain provisional context rather than the analytical anchor. [1]

The World Bank's October 2026 India Development Update projects 7.1% growth in FY2026-27, supported by domestic demand and exports, while identifying risks from oil prices, El Niño and volatile capital flows. The longer-run question is more demanding: whether investment raises the productive capacity of firms, lifts real output per worker and broadens the export base. India's domestic market provides scale; sustained income convergence requires that firms convert this scale into productivity and international competitiveness. [2]

There is evidence of a stronger capital cycle, but it should be distinguished from the investment rate. In the latest RBI table based on the 2022-23 national-accounts base, gross capital formation was 34.56% of GDP at constant prices in FY2024-25. This is a broader measure than GFCF and is the latest reported ratio in that table; the FY2025-26 entry is not yet available. Separately, the 11.9% Q1 FY2026-27 GFCF growth rate is a year-on-year flow measure, not the investment share of GDP. [6]

The semiconductor opportunity gives the demand-to-production question a concrete industrial dimension. The EY–IESA report estimates that India's semiconductor market could expand from about US$64 billion in 2026 to US$200 billion by 2035. That path implies a compound annual growth rate of approximately 13.5%, calculated from the report's endpoints over nine years. It is a projection of market demand, not a forecast of domestic production, value added or exports. NITI Aayog's May 2026 roadmap presents a separate expert-based demand trajectory, from US$44 billion in FY2026 to about US$206 billion in FY2035, implying a materially faster 19% annual rate. The different starting values, periods and forecast assumptions should not be conflated; both are scenarios for demand, not realised domestic output. [10 11]

Fabrication capacity is one part of the investment. The surrounding ecosystem includes materials, equipment, testing, packaging, process engineering, power, water, logistics, research and skilled labour. This network will determine how far semiconductor investment strengthens domestic capabilities and raises productivity in adjacent industries. The policy commitment is substantial: the Semicon 2.0 programme carries a ₹1.275 lakh crore outlay. Under the first phase, twelve manufacturing units had been approved; by the Government's 18 September 2026 update, five units had begun commercial production. These are meaningful implementation milestones, but the count is not evidence by itself of domestic value added, productivity spillovers or net job creation. The first silicon fab is scheduled for commissioning in 2028. Execution, yields, local sourcing and repeat orders will be more informative measures of industrial depth than the headline investment pipeline.

Semiconductors should also be viewed alongside India's established services advantage. The World Bank reports that employment in global capability centres rose from 1.9 million in 2024 to 2.36 million in 2025, while private AI investment increased from US$1.2 billion to US$4.1 billion. These figures point to an opportunity in higher-value technology, engineering, research and business services. The next productivity gain will depend on whether these capabilities diffuse beyond large global firms into domestic companies, smaller suppliers and public services.

What the productivity evidence currently says

The latest official data offer a more direct test of the article's thesis. The RBI's KLEMS-based growth accounting estimates attribute, on average, about 40% of GVA growth to capital, 33% to labour and 27% to total factor productivity (TFP) during 2021-22 to 2023-24. The same RBI analysis describes TFP as a key driver of aggregate GVA growth since 2007-08. This is evidence that productivity has contributed materially to recent growth; it does not establish that the latest investment surge will generate the same contribution in future. KLEMS estimates TFP after accounting for labour, capital and intermediate inputs, rather than treating all output growth as productivity. [3]

The labour input itself has a quality dimension. RBI's KLEMS-based labour accounts estimate average labour-quality growth of 0.60% a year for the total economy and 0.63% for manufacturing during 2000-01 to 2021-22. Across the full 1980-81 to 2021-22 period, employment accounted for about one-quarter of output growth and improved labour quality a further five percentage points. These are long-run growth-accounting estimates, not annual labour-productivity growth rates. They show why headcount alone is an incomplete measure of the contribution of labour. [4]

For a more recent manufacturing cross-check, MoSPI's Annual Survey of Industries (ASI) 2024-25 reports that GVA per person engaged in the manufacturing corporate sector rose by 2.25% over the previous year. The corporate sector accounted for more than 89% of manufacturing GVA. This is a nominal, establishment-based measure: it is not deflated labour productivity for the whole economy, and it should not be interpreted as a real productivity growth rate. The same MoSPI release reports corporate-sector employment growth of 7.57% and gross value of plant and machinery growth of 7.25%. The combination points to expanding capacity and employment, while the modest nominal increase in GVA per person engaged makes it important to track real output per worker as price-adjusted data become available. [7]

The RBI's July 2024 India KLEMS release covers 27 industries through 2022-23 and includes labour productivity and TFP series; it also provides a provisional total-economy productivity estimate for 2023-24. The latest release therefore does not provide a KLEMS productivity reading for 2025 or 2026. The database is the appropriate structural benchmark, but it must be read with its vintage and estimation assumptions in view. The empirical proposition to test over the next few years is whether the current capital cycle is followed by sustained gains in real output per worker and TFP, rather than only higher capital intensity or nominal value added. [5]

What the next five to ten years could look like

India's medium-term growth prospects are best assessed against its productive capacity. The IMF's 2025 Article IV framework places potential growth at around 6.5% through 2030-31, easing to 6.4% in parts of the projection period. The RBI's 102nd Survey of Professional Forecasters, conducted in September and published on 7 October 2026, puts median real GDP growth at 7.1% for FY2026-27 and 7.0% for FY2027-28. These estimates cover different horizons and concepts: the RBI survey captures near-term expectations, while potential growth is an assessment of the economy's sustainable supply-side pace. Neither forecast establishes that current investment is raising productivity; that requires subsequent outcome data. [17]

A sustained outcome above that medium-term benchmark would require more than continued capital accumulation. It would depend on faster productivity growth, greater labour-force participation, a stronger export response and the movement of workers and capital towards more productive firms and sectors. The downside risks are equally structural: weak job creation, slow firm scaling, high energy and logistics costs, climate-related supply shocks and limited access to skills or finance could leave investment returns below their potential.

Growth, inflation and the interest-rate equilibrium

A decade of strong real growth need not imply a decade of high inflation or persistently high interest rates. The more favourable configuration is one in which supply capacity expands alongside demand. Manufacturing scale, better logistics, reliable electricity, competitive services and higher agricultural productivity can reduce unit costs and ease capacity constraints. In that setting, real incomes can rise without a persistent acceleration in prices.

But the inflation path will remain exposed to food, energy and climate volatility. India's food basket, dependence on imported energy and infrastructure gaps can transmit supply shocks into headline CPI and household expectations. The RBI's inflation-targeting framework, centred on 4% CPI inflation with a tolerance band of 2–6% through March 2031, provides the nominal anchor. In its October 2026 survey, professional forecasters' ten-year inflation expectations were around 4.2%. The RBI's October policy statement nevertheless projected FY2026-27 CPI inflation at 5.2%, citing food, energy and weather-related pressures. The contrast is instructive: a credible long-run anchor can coexist with material near-term supply risks.

The policy-rate setting changed materially on 7 October 2026. The MPC raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% and shifted its stance to calibrated tightening. The resolution stated that rate cuts were off the table in the near term, with subsequent action conditional on underlying inflation, the broadening of price pressures, second-round effects from the supply shock and demand impulses. The decision followed the rise in inflation risks and should be treated as the operative monetary-policy signal in any medium-term assessment. [9 16]

A long-run nominal repo-rate path cannot be inferred from the 4% inflation target alone. It would require an explicit estimate of the equilibrium real rate, the inflation premium, fiscal and external risk premia, and the evolution of potential growth. No such neutral-rate estimate is developed here, so the article does not assign a long-run repo-rate level. For markets, the more defensible implication is conditional: a durable reduction in inflation volatility and sovereign risk premia would support lower long-term discount rates, while persistent supply shocks or a higher equilibrium real rate could keep real yields elevated.

For markets, the distinction matters. Higher nominal GDP growth can support corporate revenues, but the valuation of those cash flows also depends on the cost of capital. A durable decline in inflation volatility and sovereign risk premia could lower long-term discount rates. A growth acceleration driven by capital scarcity, energy constraints or fiscal slippage could instead coexist with higher real yields.

The semiconductor test: from investment to productivity

Semiconductors are strategically important because they sit upstream of a wide range of industries: electronics, automobiles, telecoms, industrial equipment, defence, data infrastructure and consumer devices. A domestic ecosystem can create value beyond the wafer or packaged chip itself. Design, fabrication, assembly and testing, equipment maintenance, materials, process engineering, reliability and advanced packaging generate different kinds of technical capability.

The employment case should be assessed against realised industrial outcomes rather than headline hiring forecasts. MoSPI's ASI 2024-25 results show that the registered manufacturing sector added more than 14 lakh employees, taking estimated employment above two crore; total employment rose by 7.19% and GVA by 9.59% in current prices. For the registered factory sector as a whole, dividing ASI GVA of ₹26.94 lakh crore by 2.10 crore persons engaged gives approximately ₹12.83 lakh of GVA per person engaged in FY2024-25, in current prices. This is a calculated nominal ratio, not deflated labour productivity and not a measure for the whole economy. Separately, MoSPI reports that the manufacturing corporate sector, which accounts for more than 89% of manufacturing GVA, recorded employment growth of 7.57% and nominal GVA per person engaged growth of 2.25%. These are manufacturing-wide results, not semiconductor-specific outcomes. They provide a more defensible benchmark against which future semiconductor employment and value added can be assessed. [7]

The wider productivity dividend depends on whether local suppliers learn, Indian firms move into more complex functions, technology diffuses to adjacent industries, and domestic production becomes commercially competitive. A semiconductor plant that relies heavily on imported inputs, expertise and continuing subsidies may still have strategic value, but its domestic value added and productivity spillovers will be more limited than those of a dense supplier and innovation ecosystem. Progress should therefore be assessed through realised output, local sourcing and value added, export performance, manufacturing yields, the subsidy cost per unit of additional activity, and job quality relative to a credible counterfactual. Announced capacity and projected hiring are inputs to this assessment, not evidence of the eventual economic return.

The semiconductor opportunity sits alongside India's broader manufacturing agenda. Electronics assembly, capital goods, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, auto components, renewable-energy equipment and precision engineering all contribute to industrial depth. Each requires predictable regulation, patient capital, access to export markets, reliable infrastructure and firms able to scale.

The next commodity cycle and India's industrial opportunity

The next commodity cycle is unlikely to be a uniform supercycle. Iron ore remains exposed to the structural adjustment in Chinese steel demand, while copper demand is more closely linked to electrification, grid investment, renewable power and data-centre infrastructure. The OECD's 2026 steel outlook expects Chinese steel demand to remain under pressure through 2030, while the IEA's 2026 critical-minerals outlook estimates a potential copper supply gap of around 25% by 2035 in its base case, based on existing and announced projects. These are global market assessments, not forecasts of India's commodity balance. The India-specific transmission is through import costs: RBI data put imports of crude petroleum and products at about ₹15.33 lakh crore in FY2025-26, while the broader category of metalliferous ores and other minerals amounted to about ₹1.25 lakh crore. The latter is not a copper-only measure and should not be read as one. For India, the macroeconomic issue is the pass-through from imported energy and mineral prices to the trade balance, industrial input costs and inflation. Domestic processing, recycling, material efficiency and competitive downstream engineering can improve resilience, but the case should be evaluated against actual import dependence and value added rather than the global copper narrative alone. [13 14 15]

Infrastructure is productive capacity

Infrastructure's longer-term economic value lies in lowering the cost, uncertainty and time required to move people, goods, energy, data and ideas. Freight corridors, ports, roads and urban transit can enlarge labour and product markets. Reliable electricity and grid capacity are essential for factories and data centres. Water availability, treatment and recycling are particularly important for semiconductor fabrication. Digital infrastructure can lower transaction costs, but it cannot compensate for unreliable physical networks.

Public capital expenditure can crowd in private investment when it resolves network bottlenecks and improves project returns. This is a conditional mechanism, not an automatic result: this article does not estimate the causal effect or compare capex returns with alternative public spending. The next phase therefore requires more than a high capex allocation: project selection, land acquisition, state-level execution, maintenance, urban governance and financial viability determine the return on that spending. As fiscal consolidation proceeds, the economic return on each rupee of public investment will matter increasingly, including the ability to crowd in private capital and fund maintenance. The OECD's 2026 assessment identifies infrastructure gaps, including electricity reliability, as constraints on Indian productivity and competitiveness. [18]

Climate resilience belongs within this investment agenda. Heat stress affects labour productivity and urban energy demand; monsoon variability influences agricultural output, food prices and rural incomes; and water security is material for cities, power generation and semiconductor fabrication. Grid reliability, transmission, storage, water recycling and climate-resilient agriculture are therefore productive assets, with returns that extend well beyond environmental policy.

There is also a spatial dimension. Industrial clusters can raise productivity through supplier proximity, labour-market depth and knowledge spillovers, but they can widen regional disparities if investment is concentrated in a small number of states. The ability of tier-two and tier-three cities to provide power, water, transport, housing, health and education will influence how widely the gains from industrialisation are distributed.

The binding constraint may be human development

India's demographic scale is an advantage only when people can participate productively in the economy. The IMF's productivity assessment identifies stalled manufacturing productivity, subdued firm entry and exit, and the need to move workers towards higher-productivity activities. It also points to the prevalence of small, low-productivity firms and barriers to scaling. The OECD's 2026 assessment similarly highlights skill shortages and low female labour-force participation. These are central growth constraints: capital deepening yields less when firms cannot scale, workers cannot move to better jobs or new technology remains concentrated in a small part of the economy. [18]

The semiconductor workforce is a useful microcosm. Training more engineers is necessary, but the sector also needs technicians, equipment specialists, production supervisors, quality-control professionals and experienced managers. Industry-linked curricula, apprenticeships, faculty development and access to laboratory infrastructure are as important as headline enrolment. The same principle applies to advanced manufacturing more broadly.

Human development is a direct input into the growth model. Foundational learning shapes the ability of young people to acquire technical skills. Nutrition and public health influence learning outcomes and labour productivity. Affordable urban housing and transport affect access to employment. Women's participation also depends on safe mobility, childcare, workplace design and the availability of formal jobs. The PLFS Annual Report 2025 puts female labour-force participation at 40.0% for people aged 15 and above, against 79.1% for men; the female rate was broadly unchanged from 2024. The proportion of workers in regular wage or salaried employment rose to 23.6% from 22.4%. Participation is improving over the longer term, but the gender gap and the quality of available work remain substantial challenges.

The skills pipeline is another constraint. The PLFS 2025 report finds that 4.2% of people aged 15–59 had received formal vocational or technical training. It estimates that 21.0% of people aged 15–24 and 25.0% of those aged 15–29 were not in employment, education or training, using the usual-status measure. The two age bands are distinct and should not be interchanged. The report also notes that the NEET module was introduced through a schedule modification in July 2025, while the broader PLFS sampling design was revamped from January 2025. These are useful baseline indicators, but they should not be compared mechanically with earlier-year estimates. [8]

The IMF has argued that stronger innovation and business dynamism could materially lift India's productivity growth. Its analysis points to large productivity gaps between small and large firms, limited R&D intensity and the need for firms to scale, adopt technology and reallocate resources towards more productive uses. Progress will require competition, predictable rules, easier entry and exit, stronger research links between universities and firms, deeper integration with global markets, and a financing system that backs productive risk-taking.

What to watch: the signposts for 2031 and 2036

The quality of India's growth will become clearer through a small set of observable tests. Does private investment broaden beyond a few large firms and favoured sectors? Do domestic value added, manufacturing exports and supplier capabilities rise alongside labour productivity, rather than mainly through low-value assembly? Do services exports move into higher-value activities, and does AI adoption spread beyond large firms? Do real wages and formal employment grow beyond the organised corporate sector? Does inflation volatility decline as food supply chains, energy networks and urban infrastructure improve? Finally, do learning, health, skills and female employment outcomes advance in step with capital expenditure?

These tests are connected, but their causal links should be treated as hypotheses to test rather than outcomes already demonstrated. Better human capital may raise the return on infrastructure. More reliable infrastructure makes advanced manufacturing viable. Higher productivity supports real wages and corporate earnings. Broader household income growth sustains domestic demand. A stable nominal anchor allows monetary policy to support investment without repeatedly having to suppress inflationary pressure.

The decade's central question: Can India turn the strength of its domestic market into a platform for globally competitive production, and turn that production into more productive, better-paid work? The semiconductor push offers a visible test. The answer will depend on the less visible foundations: firm-level capability, infrastructure reliability, skills, health, education and institutional execution.
