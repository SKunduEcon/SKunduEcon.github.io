Back to the Zero-Yield World? Revisiting Loeys and Kundu (2019) in a Higher-for-Longer Market

By Shiny Kundu | 21 November 2026

Bottom line. The US is a long way from the zero-yield conditions examined by Jan Loeys and Shiny Kundu in 2019. The Federal Reserve's target range is 3.75%–4.00%, the 10-year Treasury yield was 5.04% on 19 November, and inflation remains elevated. A return to zero would require a sequence of shocks: a deep demand contraction, disinflation or outright deflation, aggressive monetary easing and limited fiscal support. The sharp global sell-off in technology shares on 20 November, amid renewed doubts about AI valuations and financing, makes the investment channel especially relevant. It does not, by itself, establish that the AI capital-expenditure cycle is reversing. The 2019 framework remains useful as a stress scenario, not as the central market regime today.

What the 2019 paper set out to test

In What if the US Joins the Zero-Yield World?, published in J.P. Morgan's The Long-Term Strategist in 2019, Jan Loeys and Shiny Kundu examined whether the United States could follow Japan and parts of Europe into a prolonged period of near-zero government bond yields. The analysis asked what combination of macroeconomic conditions and policy responses could produce that outcome, and how investors might need to reposition across the transition.

The framework was built around a combination of low starting yields and global forces depressing equilibrium interest rates; a deflationary shock severe enough to push policy rates to zero or below; large-scale asset purchases; inflation and inflation expectations close to zero; markets questioning whether central banks had further effective tools; weak financial intermediaries; and limited capacity or political appetite for fiscal expansion. These conditions were interconnected. A recession alone would not be sufficient if inflation expectations remained anchored above zero, monetary policy retained credibility and fiscal authorities could support demand.

The US entry scenario considered an extended trade conflict and uncertainty-driven collapse in capital expenditure. Such a shock could trigger recession and falling inflation, prompting the Federal Reserve to cut rates to zero and restart quantitative easing. A failure of Congress to deliver fiscal support would deepen the adjustment. The paper treated this as a risk scenario, not its baseline forecast.

The analysis also distinguished between two phases. Entry is the transition from positive yields towards zero, typically during a deflationary shock when risk assets are under pressure. Life is the subsequent period in which the economy and markets adapt to a low-yield environment. The distinction matters because the portfolio response during a recessionary transition need not resemble the allocation that performs best once near-zero rates become persistent.

What the original portfolio analysis implied

The 2019 exercise used a five-year horizon and an 8% portfolio-risk constraint to compare allocations across three scenarios. Its optimiser produced materially different portfolios depending on whether yields remained unchanged, fell towards zero or stayed near zero. The expected returns and weights below are outputs of that historical model, not current forecasts or investment recommendations.

In the Entry phase, the paper favoured long duration, a short US dollar and lower exposure to risk assets. In the Life phase, it favoured credit and equities with an income orientation, including REITs, high-dividend stocks, high-yield credit and unhedged emerging-market local debt. It was cautious on financials, commodities and Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities.

One of the paper's more important empirical observations was that the move to zero yields in Japan, Europe and Switzerland did not produce a lasting, broad-based inflation of equity and credit valuations. Credit spreads initially widened during the deflationary shock and subsequently settled. Households did not abandon bonds, while financials underperformed. The result cautioned against assuming that lower government yields mechanically translate into higher equity valuations.

The US market in November 2026: a different starting point

The current policy and yield environment is almost the inverse of the 2019 entry conditions. The Federal Reserve raised its target range by 25 basis points in September 2026 to 3.75%–4.00%. At its 27–28 October meeting, it held the range unchanged in a 10–2 vote; the two dissenting members preferred another 25-basis-point increase. The published minutes recorded that futures markets assigned a high probability to a further increase by year-end, while the Desk survey median also anticipated a December hike and another in March 2027. This is a market debating the extent of further tightening, not the timing of a return to zero.

Treasury yields reflect that repricing. On 19 November, the two-year yield stood at 4.76%, the 10-year at 5.04% and the 30-year at 5.40%. The 10-year yield was approximately 90 basis points above its level a year earlier. The two-year/10-year spread was positive by about 28 basis points. Long yields had eased from their mid-November highs, but remained elevated.

Inflation is also inconsistent with a zero-yield transition. The October 2026 CPI rose 0.4% month on month, while the index excluding food and energy increased 0.3%. BLS could not publish October's year-on-year CPI changes for most series because it had not collected October 2025 survey data during the lapse in appropriations. The Federal Reserve's October minutes reported staff estimates of September PCE inflation at 3.6% headline and 3.1% core. The immediate policy problem is persistent inflation, not entrenched deflation.

The labour market has softened only gradually. October non-farm payrolls increased by 165,000 and unemployment was 4.1%, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The Federal Reserve described activity as expanding at a strong pace, with resilient domestic spending and robust business investment. A sharp contraction in employment and demand, of the kind needed to drive policy rates towards zero, is not visible in these indicators.

What could bring the zero-yield scenario back?

The 2019 framework is most useful when translated into sequences of events rather than single market triggers. A fall in oil prices, a correction in technology shares or one weak payroll report would not, by itself, recreate the conditions for zero yields. The following channels would matter if they combined to produce a broad demand shock and a persistent fall in inflation.

1. An AI investment cycle that turns into a capital-expenditure bust

The 2019 paper's own US example involved uncertainty causing firms to defer capital expenditure. The AI infrastructure build-out creates a contemporary version of that vulnerability. The Federal Reserve's October minutes noted rapid growth in debt associated with AI investment, including off-balance-sheet leasing commitments and circular financing arrangements. Staff warned that disappointing returns could amplify losses through asset-price declines and deleveraging. The minutes also recorded wider credit spreads for high-yield bonds financing data-centre construction and greater investor scrutiny of hyperscaler funding requirements.

There is an important counterpoint: demand for exposure to the AI theme remains substantial. Anthropic priced its November IPO at US$83 a share, above the marketed US$70–78 range. Strong primary-market demand can coexist with tighter financing conditions for less profitable infrastructure projects. The signpost is not a single share-price correction; it is a sustained reduction in cloud capital-expenditure guidance, rising refinancing costs and a widening of credit spreads across AI-linked borrowers. If investment were cut abruptly, the spillover to equipment orders, construction, power infrastructure and technology employment could become macroeconomically significant.

2. Monetary tightening meets a weakening demand cycle

A second route would be a policy mistake in the presence of a supply shock. If energy or tariff-related inflation keeps policy restrictive while household spending and business investment lose momentum, the lagged effects of higher interest rates could become more visible just as the original price shock fades. Housing, smaller businesses and leveraged borrowers would be particularly sensitive. The transition from inflation concern to demand weakness could then be rapid.

3. A sharp energy-price reversal

A durable easing of geopolitical risk could lower oil prices and headline inflation. The disinflationary impulse would be faster if freight, diesel and other input costs also eased. The macroeconomic effect would depend on timing: lower energy prices alongside resilient demand would be supportive, whereas a sharp fall in prices accompanied by collapsing orders, employment and inflation expectations would reinforce a broader deflationary shock.

4. Financial stress in leveraged intermediaries and credit markets

The 2019 paper identified weak financial intermediaries as one of the conditions that could magnify a downturn. The current areas to monitor include leveraged non-bank finance, private credit, fund liquidity and the financing structures supporting data-centre construction. The Federal Reserve's October financial-stability discussion highlighted hedge-fund leverage, opaque AI-related financing and the possibility that asset sales could amplify shocks. It also judged the banking system to be well capitalised and the financial system resilient overall. The distinction is important: these are identified vulnerabilities and amplification channels, not evidence that a systemic crisis is already under way.

5. Fiscal constraints during a recession

The final condition is a limited fiscal response. A recession would be more difficult to arrest if high public debt, rising interest costs or political disagreement constrained discretionary support. The Federal Reserve's minutes explicitly noted the interaction between government indebtedness, rising long-term yields and financial stability, and the possibility that diminished fiscal space could complicate the response to adverse shocks. Fiscal constraint would not cause the downturn on its own, but it could lengthen the period in which monetary policy is required to carry the burden.

Portfolio implications: entry is not life

If the chain of events began to unfold, the distinction between Entry and Life would again become central. During Entry, the priority in the 2019 framework was protection against a deflationary shock: longer-duration high-grade bonds, reduced exposure to cyclical risk and a short-dollar position. During Life, after yields had reached very low levels and the initial shock had passed, the framework shifted towards income-generating credit and equities.

Those historical allocations should not be lifted mechanically into today's portfolio decisions. A 10-year Treasury yield around 5% offers meaningful carry, but duration can still lose value if inflation remains persistent or term premia rise. Conversely, a sharp disinflationary slowdown would improve the potential hedge offered by high-quality government bonds. Credit spreads, funding conditions and the source of any equity-market weakness would help distinguish a temporary correction from the start of a regime change.

The paper's central lesson remains relevant: a fall in government-bond yields does not automatically produce an equity or credit rally. In the Entry phase, spreads can widen and earnings expectations can deteriorate before lower discount rates become supportive. The performance of financials and income strategies can also differ substantially between the transition and the prolonged low-yield state.

Signposts worth monitoring

The following are analytical signposts, not model-derived thresholds or trading signals:

  • The policy-rate path: a sustained shift in futures pricing from hikes towards cuts, accompanied by a fall in the two-year Treasury yield below the prevailing federal-funds target range.
  • Inflation breadth: a persistent decline in monthly core inflation and medium-term inflation expectations, rather than a temporary fall in headline energy prices.
  • Labour-market deterioration: a sustained rise in unemployment, weaker payroll growth and falling job openings across a broad range of sectors.
  • AI investment and credit: material reductions in hyperscaler capital-expenditure plans, widening spreads on AI-linked borrowing and evidence of refinancing stress.
  • Financial-system amplification: forced asset sales, tighter credit availability or liquidity pressures spreading from non-bank intermediaries into broader funding markets.
  • Fiscal response: evidence that debt-service costs or political constraints are limiting the scale and speed of countercyclical support.

A single weak equity session or a brief decline in Treasury yields would not establish a return to the zero-yield regime. The relevant signal would be a reinforcing sequence: investment and demand weaken, labour-market conditions deteriorate, inflation falls persistently, and monetary easing is no longer sufficient without fiscal support.

The zero-yield world remains a tail scenario

The structural forces discussed in 2019, including ageing populations, weak productivity growth and subdued demand for capital, have not disappeared. Their influence can be obscured for periods by fiscal expansion, supply constraints, geopolitical shocks and investment booms. If those cyclical forces fade, the longer-run equilibrium-rate debate may return. But the route from today's 5% long bond yield to zero would require a major change in the macroeconomic sequence, not simply a change in market sentiment.

For now, the 2019 paper is best read as a scenario framework rather than a forecast. Its value lies in clarifying the conditions under which duration, credit, equities and currencies behave differently, and in reminding investors that the portfolio which protects capital during a recession may not be the one that performs best after a prolonged low-yield regime has become established.
