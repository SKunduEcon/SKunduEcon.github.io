60/40 in a Higher-for-Longer World: When the Old Allocation Works Again, and When It Doesn't

By Shiny Kundu · 7 November 2026

Bottom line. Higher yields have restored an important part of the traditional 60/40 portfolio's appeal: bonds once again offer meaningful income and a more credible prospective return. But a higher starting yield does not automatically restore the bond hedge. The allocation works best when inflation is contained and weaker growth allows yields to fall. It is much less reliable when yields rise because of persistent inflation, adverse supply shocks or a higher term premium.

The return of the bond allocation

For much of the decade following the Global Financial Crisis, the traditional 60/40 portfolio faced an uncomfortable arithmetic. With government bond yields compressed, the fixed-income allocation offered limited income and diminishing scope for capital appreciation. Investors were being asked to accept duration risk without much compensation.

That calculation has changed. On 6 November 2026, the US 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.878% after October payrolls increased by 165,000, well above the 65,000 consensus estimate. The unemployment rate edged down to 4.1%. The data reinforced expectations that the Federal Reserve could keep policy restrictive, with markets assigning a higher probability to another rate increase. The immediate message was one of labour-market resilience and persistent policy restraint, rather than imminent easing.

At these yield levels, bonds offer a more substantial income cushion than they did in the zero-yield era. If yields subsequently decline, investors also have scope for capital appreciation. The opportunity cost of holding high-quality fixed income has therefore fallen, strengthening the strategic case for a balanced portfolio.

But the source of the yield increase matters. A rise driven by stronger real growth is not the same as one driven by inflation risk, fiscal concerns or a higher term premium. The first can coexist with healthy corporate earnings; the others can pressure both sides of a balanced portfolio.

What the original research argued

In June 2020, 60/40 in a Zero-Yield World, a J.P. Morgan Global Markets Strategy paper co-authored by Shiny Kundu, examined the implications of a prolonged low-yield environment for long-term asset allocation.

The starting point was the historical performance of a conventional US portfolio comprising 60% S&P 500 equities and 40% US Aggregate bonds. Between 1983 and 2019, the annually rebalanced portfolio delivered a compound annual return of approximately 9.9%, with annual volatility of 10.6%.

But the forward-looking arithmetic had deteriorated. With the US Aggregate yield near 1.3% in June 2020, the paper estimated a return of around 1% over the following decade. Its equity return assumption was approximately 5%, producing a projected return of roughly 3.4% for the conventional 60/40 portfolio.

The concern extended beyond returns. In a world where bond yields were already close to their lower bound, government bonds had less room to rally during an equity sell-off. Their traditional role as portfolio insurance was therefore less dependable.

The paper explored an alternative: broadening the opportunity set to include assets between conventional fixed income and equities. These included high-yield bonds and loans, collateralised loan obligations, commercial mortgage-backed securities, convertibles, REITs, utilities and preferred shares. The underlying idea was to distinguish short-term volatility from longer-horizon uncertainty. Some credit and hybrid assets can experience substantial interim price movements, yet their returns may be more closely linked to cyclical credit losses and spread normalisation over longer holding periods.

Its illustrative allocations included a 20/40/40 portfolio of conventional bonds, hybrid assets and equities for investors concerned about interim drawdowns, and a 60/40 allocation to hybrids and equities for investors with a greater focus on long-term risk and return. This was not a universal recommendation to abandon government bonds. It was a case for reassessing the contribution of each asset class as the macroeconomic regime changed.

Why higher yields strengthen the case for 60/40

Income provides a more substantial return cushion. A bond's starting yield is an important determinant of its prospective return over a sufficiently long holding period, assuming credit quality and duration remain broadly stable. A higher starting yield improves the income component and offers some protection against moderate further increases in yields, although it does not eliminate mark-to-market losses.

The opportunity cost of holding bonds has declined. When yields were close to zero, investors had little income to compensate them for duration risk. At materially higher yields, high-quality fixed income can compete more credibly with other income-producing assets. It also provides liquidity and a potential source of funds for rebalancing during equity-market weakness.

Capital appreciation is possible again. If inflation moderates and monetary policy eventually eases, yields can decline and bond prices can rise. A balanced portfolio can then benefit from both the income earned during the holding period and capital gains generated by falling yields.

This is particularly relevant in a growth slowdown accompanied by disinflation. Weaker activity can weigh on corporate earnings while giving central banks room to reduce policy rates. Government bonds can then perform their traditional defensive function.

When 60/40 does not work as intended

The weakness in a conventional 60/40 portfolio is not necessarily the allocation itself. It is the assumption that its two components will reliably offset each other when markets come under pressure. That relationship is regime-dependent.

1. Persistent inflation and adverse supply shocks

When inflation is above target and supply shocks threaten to become persistent, central banks may have limited room to ease policy even as growth weakens. Inflation expectations and bond yields can rise at the same time that equity valuations come under pressure. This is the difficult combination for a traditional 60/40 portfolio: equities decline on weaker growth or lower margins, while bonds decline because investors demand greater inflation compensation or expect tighter monetary policy.

A Federal Reserve note published on 16 October 2026 by Canlin Li and Emre Yoldas documents how the hedging properties of government bonds depend on inflation dynamics and the type of shock hitting the economy. Its analysis finds that stock-bond correlations have returned to negative territory in the post-2021 period across the United States, euro area and United Kingdom, in contrast to the strongly positive correlations observed during 2000–2021. In the US, the reported correlation was 0.40 in 2000–2021 and -0.09 after 2021. In the paper's convention, a negative correlation indicates that falling stock prices can coincide with rising bond yields, weakening the bond hedge.

2. Rising term premia and fiscal concerns

A higher yield is not always a benign development. If long-term yields rise because investors demand greater compensation for duration, fiscal uncertainty or the supply of government debt, the increase can translate into capital losses for existing bondholders. Higher yields improve prospective returns for new investors, but they do not immediately protect portfolios already exposed to long-duration securities. The path matters as much as the destination.

3. Equity valuations remain vulnerable

Higher real risk-free rates raise the discount rate applied to future corporate cash flows. This can pressure equity valuations, particularly where market prices depend heavily on distant earnings expectations. A 60/40 portfolio may therefore experience simultaneous losses if yields rise because of higher real rates or inflation risk, rather than stronger productivity and sustainable growth.

4. Credit is not a substitute for sovereign duration

High-yield bonds and leveraged loans may offer attractive income, but their credit spreads can widen sharply during recessions and periods of financial stress. They may diversify a portfolio over some longer horizons, but they are not equivalent to government bonds in a flight to quality. Replacing the entire defensive allocation with credit can leave a portfolio exposed to the same underlying growth and liquidity shock as equities.

The composition of the 40 matters again

The debate should move beyond whether 60/40 is back. The more useful question is what belongs in the fixed-income allocation, and what role each instrument is expected to play.

Government bonds can provide duration exposure, liquidity and potential protection during disinflationary slowdowns. Shorter-duration securities can reduce sensitivity to further increases in yields, while floating-rate instruments can offer income with less direct exposure to changes in benchmark rates.

Investment-grade credit adds carry, but introduces spread and downgrade risk. High-yield bonds and leveraged loans can increase income potential, though their cyclical sensitivity makes them less reliable as defensive assets. Securitised credit, preferred securities and other hybrid instruments can broaden the opportunity set, but require careful assessment of liquidity, leverage, structure and underlying exposures.

This is consistent with the original paper's emphasis on the hybrid space between conventional bonds and equities. The important qualification is that diversification benefits must be evaluated over the relevant investment horizon, and not inferred from a long-term average alone.

A regime-based framework for long-term allocation

Market regimePortfolio implication
Disinflationary slowdownConventional 60/40 can be effective as government bonds regain defensive value.
Resilient growth, contained inflationHigher bond income supports the allocation, while equities retain earnings-growth potential.
Persistent inflation or supply shockStock-bond diversification can weaken; inflation protection and duration management become more relevant.
Fiscal or term-premium shockLong-duration government bonds can suffer alongside equities; liquidity and duration management matter.
Recession with widening credit spreadsHigh-yield and leveraged credit may amplify losses; sovereign duration and credit quality become more important.

The framework also highlights why a single allocation cannot serve every investor equally well. Liquidity needs, liabilities, investment horizon, drawdown tolerance, currency exposure and the ability to rebalance all influence the appropriate balance between equities, government bonds and hybrid assets.

The return of 60/40, with a qualification

The zero-yield environment exposed the limitations of a portfolio whose defensive allocation offered little income and limited scope for capital appreciation. Higher yields have improved that arithmetic. The conventional 60/40 portfolio once again has a credible income proposition, and the possibility of a bond rally provides an additional source of diversification when inflation is contained and growth weakens.

But higher yields alone do not restore the old portfolio regime. The source of the yield increase matters. A rise driven by stronger real growth is different from one driven by persistent inflation, fiscal risk or a higher term premium. The former can coexist with healthy corporate earnings; the latter can put pressure on both sides of a balanced portfolio.

The more useful question is therefore not whether 60/40 is dead or alive. It is whether the macroeconomic conditions underpinning its return and diversification assumptions are likely to persist. In a higher-for-longer world, the case for holding bonds has strengthened. The case for assuming that bonds will always hedge equities has not.
