Beyond the Sell-off: Reading the Valuation Signals

By Shiny Kundu | 13 November 2026

Bottom line. The sharp US equity sell-off in the first week of November was a short-horizon repricing, concentrated in technology and AI-linked stocks. It should not be confused with a change in the market's ten-year return prospects. Those depend on the valuation paid for earnings, the durability and growth of those earnings, the price of competing assets and the extent to which today's elevated multiples normalise. The framework developed by Kundu and Loeys offers a way to examine those forces without treating any single valuation ratio as a forecast.

A market correction is not a ten-year forecast

The first week of November brought a sharp reversal in US equities. On 5 November, the Nasdaq Composite entered correction territory, while semiconductor shares bore a particularly heavy share of the decline. The immediate concerns centred on the scale and prospective returns of AI infrastructure investment, the possibility of slower data-centre expansion and the crowded positioning that had accumulated around a narrow group of technology leaders. The S&P 500 lost 2.7% over the week to 6 November, while the Nasdaq fell 3.4%.

By 12 November, the major indices had recovered some ground. Reuters' assessment of positioning, options activity, correlations and technical indicators was mixed: some signs suggested that crowded technology exposure had been reduced, but there was no unambiguous signal that volatility had run its course. These are useful observations about market structure and near-term risk. They are not, in themselves, evidence that the long-term expected return on US equities has materially changed.

A correction changes the price at which investors own future cash flows. Whether it changes the ten-year return outlook materially depends on the scale of the price adjustment relative to earnings, dividends and the discount rate. A 10% fall in an expensive market can leave valuations expensive. Equally, a relatively modest decline can improve prospective returns if it coincides with a durable improvement in earnings or a decline in the required return on equity.

The Kundu–Loeys framework: eight signals, one long horizon

In Empirical Models of Long-term US Equity Returns (1 March 2021), Kundu and Loeys examined eight valuation and positioning measures, together with a simple building-block model based on the reversion of earnings towards trend, the valuation multiple towards its long-run average and the dividend yield. The objective was not to call market tops or bottoms. It was to estimate the compound nominal total return on the S&P 500 over the subsequent ten years, and to assess the uncertainty around those estimates.

The eight signals capture different dimensions of valuation:

  1. Trailing operating P/E. The price investors pay for current operating earnings. A high multiple leaves less room for future returns from multiple expansion and makes the outcome more dependent on earnings growth.
  2. Nominal earnings yield. The inverse of the P/E ratio, adjusted by ten-year inflation. It places the starting earnings yield in a nominal-return framework.
  3. CAPE. The price relative to ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. Smoothing earnings across a cycle reduces the influence of unusually strong or weak annual profits.
  4. Excess CAPE yield. The cyclically adjusted earnings yield less the real Treasury yield. This recognises that equity valuations cannot be assessed independently of the return available on government bonds.
  5. Dividend yield. The cash distribution relative to the price paid. The starting yield is one component of long-run total return, alongside earnings growth and changes in valuation.
  6. Household equity holdings. Equities as a share of household financial assets. This captures both valuation and the extent to which household portfolios are already exposed to the asset class.
  7. Market capitalisation to GDP. The Buffett Indicator, comparing aggregate market value with the size of the domestic economy. The global revenue base of US-listed companies complicates a literal domestic comparison.
  8. Tobin's Q. Market value relative to the replacement value of corporate assets. It provides an asset-based perspective on how much investors are paying for the underlying capital stock.

The signals are related, but not interchangeable. P/E and CAPE focus on the price paid for earnings; dividend yield captures cash income; household allocations add investor positioning; market-cap-to-GDP relates the market to the economy; and excess CAPE yield introduces the opportunity cost of bonds. Their differences matter most when they disagree.

Why out-of-sample performance matters

A valuation ratio can appear highly informative when fitted to the full historical sample and still perform poorly when used in real time. Kundu and Loeys therefore estimated the relationships recursively, beginning in 1982. Each forecast used only information that would have been available at the time, and was subsequently compared with the S&P 500 total return actually earned over the following decade.

This exercise qualified the conventional emphasis on CAPE and the Buffett Indicator. Both had strong in-sample relationships with future returns, but their out-of-sample forecast errors were materially higher than those of several alternatives. Household equity allocations performed best over the full test period, although the paper noted that this signal had begun to under-predict subsequent returns in the more recent part of the sample.

The preferred composite averaged five signals: household equity holdings, trailing operating P/E, nominal earnings yield, excess CAPE yield and the trend-earnings/multiple model. The five-signal combination produced a 3.5 percentage-point root mean squared forecast error in the historical recursive exercise. At the end of 2020, it implied approximately 4% annualised nominal total returns over 2020–30. That was a forecast made with end-2020 data, not a standing estimate for the decade now underway.

The rationale for combining signals was not simply to average different valuation ratios. Historical forecast errors were less correlated than the underlying signals themselves. A combination could therefore reduce model-specific error, while avoiding excessive dependence on a single measure whose relationship with returns might change across regimes.

What the valuation dashboard says in late 2026

The latest available observations continue to describe a richly valued US equity market. They also show why a single ratio is insufficient. The CAPE reading was 40.71 in October, above its already elevated level a year earlier. The excess CAPE yield adds a different perspective. At 0.56%, it was substantially below its long-run average, suggesting that the relative compensation for holding equities rather than real government bonds was limited. This is not the same as saying equities must fall. It does mean investors are accepting a relatively small valuation cushion against disappointment.

Household equity exposure reinforces the point about positioning, but it is not a timing indicator. High allocations can persist for years when earnings growth, liquidity and risk appetite remain supportive. Similarly, the Buffett Indicator is informative as a broad valuation measure, but it is not a clean comparison between US-listed companies and US GDP: large US corporations derive substantial revenue outside the United States.

What does this imply for the decade to 2036?

The direction of the evidence is clearer than a precise point forecast. Elevated CAPE, a low excess CAPE yield, high household equity allocations and a thin dividend yield all argue for more modest long-run returns than investors would infer by extrapolating the exceptional performance of the preceding decade. They do not establish that the next decade must deliver negative returns. The outcome remains sensitive to earnings growth, profit margins, inflation, real interest rates, buybacks and the multiple investors are willing to pay in 2036.

It would be misleading to label a number calculated from today's headline ratios as the Kundu–Loeys forecast for 2026–36. The original paper estimated separate recursive relationships for each signal and selected its composite on the basis of historical out-of-sample performance and forecast-error correlations. A current ten-year estimate requires those regressions to be re-estimated on an updated data set, with consistent definitions and a common valuation date. The historical 4% estimate cannot simply be rolled forward, and the five current indicator readings in the table are not sufficient to reproduce it.

That distinction is substantive, not semantic. A useful 2036 estimate should report the five individual model forecasts, the composite, and a forecast-error range. It should also test whether the original relationships remain stable as the earnings composition of the index, the international exposure of US companies and the weight of intangible capital have changed. Until that recalibration is completed, the defensible conclusion is directional: the starting valuation signals are demanding, and the prospective return cushion appears limited, but the magnitude of the ten-year return cannot be responsibly stated from the dashboard alone.

The earnings question behind the valuation question

There is a further reason not to read high multiples as a self-contained bearish signal. The S&P 500 is not the same corporate universe it was several decades ago. Its sector composition, overseas revenue exposure, capital intensity, margins and concentration have changed. Expected productivity gains from AI could support earnings and justify some of the premium attached to leading firms. But the investment cycle also raises questions about depreciation, power and data-centre costs, competitive intensity and the eventual return on invested capital.

For a long-horizon investor, the issue is not whether AI-related shares fell sharply in one week. It is whether the earnings and cash flows currently embedded in market prices can be delivered over a full cycle. A repricing caused by a temporary positioning unwind is different from one caused by a downward revision to sustainable margins or returns on capital. The distinction will matter much more to a 2036 investor than the precise date on which the November correction found a floor.

Valuation is a compass, not a trading signal

The contribution of Kundu and Loeys is not a claim that valuation ratios can identify the next correction. It is a disciplined way of thinking about the return available from the price paid today, while recognising that different measures capture different risks and that their forecasting performance changes over time.

In November 2026, the short-term market narrative is about AI investment, concentration and positioning. The longer-term valuation narrative is about the price of earnings, the breadth of investor exposure and the relative return on bonds. The two interact, but they operate on different clocks. A correction may improve the entry point without resolving the valuation problem; strong earnings may sustain expensive multiples for longer than history would suggest.

For the decade to 2036, the appropriate question is therefore not whether last week's sell-off was the beginning of a bear market. It is what a diversified set of valuation signals, tested against their real-time forecasting record, says about the return investors can reasonably expect from today's starting point.
