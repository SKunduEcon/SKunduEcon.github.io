Stress-Testing the AI Investment Boom

By Shiny Kundu · 24 November 2026 · Updated 26 November 2026 · Markets, governance and investment risk

Investment judgment. The AI build-out is supported by substantial near-term demand, but that does not yet establish that the capital committed across the ecosystem will earn attractive returns. Those returns depend on whether recurring end-customer cash flows catch up with infrastructure spending, and whether financing remains resilient if they do not. Huang's return restores leadership continuity at Nvidia; it does not resolve questions about board independence, succession or the sector's financing structure. This is not a conclusion that AI demand is illusory or that the cycle is necessarily a bubble. It is a test of who captures durable cash flows, at what capital cost, and under what governance.

A leadership reversal with market-wide consequences

On 23 November, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had been reinstated as president, chief executive and a member of its board, effective immediately. The board said it had voted unanimously after discussions with Huang and had received input from senior levels of the US government. It also said its independent-director committee investigation would continue, alongside a newly formed National Security and Export Control Committee and the search for a chief compliance officer. These details matter: reinstatement is a board decision, while the investigations and compliance work remain open processes. Nvidia's announcement sets out the company's position.

The reversal followed Huang's resignation earlier in November and the board's initial decision not to bring him back. President Donald Trump subsequently said he had intervened with the board and taken a role in securing Huang's return. The sequence has therefore raised questions about board independence and the relationship between corporate decisions, national security assessments and political influence. Those questions should be examined separately from the personal allegations that preceded Huang's resignation. Allegations are not findings, and Nvidia has said its committee investigation will proceed to a conclusion. Reuters' account of the reinstatement describes the reversal and the governance concerns it has prompted.

The market response was sharp, but it was not a Nvidia-only event. On 23 November, the S&P 500 rose 4.73%, the Nasdaq Composite gained 6.51%, the Dow advanced 3.01% and the Russell 2000 rose 3.40%. A contemporaneous closing-market count recorded 417 S&P 500 advancers against 83 decliners, indicating a broad rebound. Nvidia nevertheless outperformed sharply: its shares rose 31.5%, adding approximately $950 billion to its market capitalisation in one session. That is a striking measure of the market's sensitivity to leadership news, but not a clean estimate of a Jensen Huang-specific premium. The move followed a severe sell-off, futures had signalled a rebound before the open, and gains extended well beyond Nvidia. The 20 November decline also reflected broader concerns about AI financing and returns, regulatory and export-control risks, an SEC accounting inquiry, and a sharp rise in the market-implied probability of a December Fed rate increase. The ECB's November financial-stability analysis adds context to the financing debate; it should not be treated as proof of a single-session market catalyst. AP's 20 November market-close report, AP's 23 November market-close report, the 23 November market-breadth note and MarketWatch's report on Nvidia's market-value gain provide further detail.

The reversal did not hold. On 24 November, the Nasdaq fell 1.48% to 23,733.54 and the S&P 500 lost 0.50% to 7,201.09; Nvidia fell 5.9%, while the Dow rose 0.85%. On 25 November, the S&P 500 declined a further 0.25% and the Nasdaq 0.62%, with AI shares retreating for a second session. At the 23 November close, the S&P 500 and Nasdaq remained about 7.4% and 12.7% below their 6 October records. By 25 November, those drawdowns had widened to approximately 8.1% and 14.5%. The sequence is more informative than either day's rally or decline in isolation: leadership news can amplify index moves, but it does not remove the macroeconomic, financing and valuation forces acting on the wider market. Reuters' 24 November market report, AP's 25 November market-close report and AP's 6 October record-close report provide the relevant data.

The founder premium is also a concentration risk

Huang's role in Nvidia's rise is difficult to separate from the company's strategic position. His technical understanding, product judgement, customer relationships and ability to coordinate a complex supply chain have helped make Nvidia a central supplier to the AI industry. That is an economic asset. It can also become a source of institutional vulnerability when investors, employees and customers begin to treat one executive as indispensable.

A company can have an exceptional founder and still require a board capable of exercising independent judgement. The relevant test is not whether the board can restore continuity after a crisis. It is whether it can demonstrate that decisions on leadership, risk and accountability follow a credible process, including when the executive concerned is unusually valuable to the business.

Nvidia's announcement contains two distinct signals. The board expressed confidence in Huang's ability to lead, while also confirming that its independent investigation and national-security oversight structures would continue. The second signal should not be lost in the relief surrounding the first. Strong governance is demonstrated through transparent processes, clear accountability and the capacity to challenge management, not simply through the return of a celebrated executive.

For investors, key-person risk is not confined to the possibility of another resignation. It can affect succession planning, strategic continuity, employee retention, customer confidence and the board's willingness to confront difficult trade-offs. The 31.5% one-day share-price rise and roughly $950 billion increase in market value on 23 November show the scale of the market response to the leadership reversal. But the rally was accompanied by a broad advance across US equities and followed an unusually sharp decline. It should be read as evidence of how heavily expectations were concentrated around the leadership event, not as a standalone valuation of Huang's contribution. A leadership premium should be assessed alongside the institutional capacity that supports it.

AI demand is real. The distribution of returns is less certain

The investment case for AI does not rest on enthusiasm alone. J.P. Morgan Asset Management's 14 November Eye on the Market note, The Icarus Files: What's next for Nvidia and AI, reports Nvidia gross margins of 75% and operating margins of 66% over 2023-2025. These figures point to an exceptionally profitable business with substantial current economic rents. The margin, circular-financing and compute-agreement estimates attributed to this note below are J.P. Morgan Asset Management's published estimates; the linked landing page may not expose the underlying report and charts to all readers, so the figures should be read as attributed research estimates rather than independently reproducible calculations.

Yet the economics of the AI ecosystem are not uniform. Chip designers, foundries, cloud providers, data-centre developers, power suppliers and model developers occupy different positions in the value chain. A supplier can earn high margins while its customers absorb rising capital expenditure. A cloud provider can report strong demand while financing capacity ahead of utilisation. A model developer can attract users without yet establishing a durable route to positive cash flow.

Competition adds another layer. Custom accelerators, alternative chip architectures and in-house silicon can gradually alter pricing power even if aggregate demand for computing continues to rise. Announced compute agreements and planned data-centre capacity are not equivalent to realised revenue, cash collection or attractive returns on invested capital. The investment question is how much of today's demand will persist when customers become more selective about utilisation and returns.

The financing structure matters as much as the spending

The European Central Bank's November 2026 analysis of AI debt financing gives this question a financial-stability dimension. Its estimate of more than $4 trillion in off-balance-sheet commitments refers to the eight-company sample in Chart A: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, Oracle, Broadcom and Anthropic. The data are as of the second quarter of 2026, with Anthropic figures drawn from its S-1 filing. The total combines fixed commitments, including future lease payments and contractual purchases, with contingent commitments such as backstops and purchase obligations that become payable if specified conditions occur. These are debt-like in economic terms, but are not $4 trillion of conventional outstanding debt, do not all relate exclusively to AI and will not necessarily fall due at the same time. The ECB separately uses a different eight-firm sample for its credit-spread comparison; the two samples should not be confused.

The ECB also describes circular links across the AI supply chain. Chipmakers and other suppliers may invest in AI companies or provide financing, while those companies use capital to purchase chips, cloud services or infrastructure. Such arrangements can support genuine investment and accelerate capacity creation. They can also make reported demand more dependent on the financing decisions of firms within the same network.

The distinction is between demand ultimately supported by independent customers and demand sustained by successive rounds of capital, credit and contractual commitments. Michael Cembalest's 14 November chart in J.P. Morgan's Eye on the Market classifies 26% of the value of investment deals in AI firms during 2021-2025 as circular AI-to-AI deals. The chart cites BIS data alongside JPMAM. This is not a standalone J.P. Morgan Asset Management estimate. The BIS's own October bulletin reports a related but differently defined measure: 28.7% of AI-firm investment deals by value involved an AI target, and 46.4% of those AI-to-AI deals also had commercial supply-chain relationships. These figures should not be conflated. Neither measure represents the share of all AI revenue or capital expenditure. In circular arrangements, a supplier or investor may finance an AI company that then spends part of the capital on chips, cloud capacity or infrastructure supplied by firms within the same network. This does not establish fictitious sales. It does mean that the durability of demand depends partly on the continued availability of financing across connected counterparties. If customer revenues disappoint, stress can travel through lease payments, guarantees, infrastructure loans and supplier investments. Apparent diversification across a portfolio may offer less protection when several holdings depend on the same underlying source of cash flow. BIS Bulletin 137 provides the underlying analysis; the Cembalest chart shows the 26% breakdown.

The funding mix is changing, but the leverage picture is not uniform. FactSet's 23 July analysis covers five hyperscalers: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle. It estimates that incremental debt rose from 9% of capex in FY2024 to 32% over the twelve months to mid-2026. That is incremental debt relative to capital expenditure over the periods analysed, not the share of all AI investment financed by debt. FactSet also finds that total debt/EBITDA remains around 1x or below for four of the five companies, with lower net leverage after cash reserves; Oracle is the outlier. FactSet expects FY2026 free cash flow to approach zero or turn negative for all except Alphabet and Microsoft. The evidence therefore points to rising external financing and pressure on internally generated cash, but not to uniform balance-sheet distress. The larger four retain meaningful rating headroom, while Oracle's leverage and contractual obligations warrant closer scrutiny. FactSet's analysis sets out the company coverage and leverage comparisons.

Debt is not inherently destabilising. Long-lived productive assets can reasonably be financed over time. The vulnerability emerges when fixed payment obligations meet uncertain utilisation, when projects require repeated refinancing, or when the economics depend on assumptions about future demand that have not yet been tested.

Power, utilisation and the gap between capacity and cash flow

AI infrastructure requires more than advanced processors. It depends on electricity, cooling, networking, land, buildings and reliable grid connections. Scarcity in any one of these inputs can raise the value of existing operational capacity while delaying projects that have been announced but cannot yet serve customers.

This creates a distinction between planned capacity and productive capacity. A data centre under construction does not generate the same cash flow as one operating at high utilisation. A power reservation is not a completed connection. A long-term customer commitment can support financing, but the quality of that commitment depends on the counterparty's ability to pay and the contractual terms if demand changes.

The physical market offers evidence that demand is not merely a story told by chip orders. JLL's August 2026 review put North American data-centre vacancy at around 1% for a third consecutive year. It recorded 25 GW of absorption in the first half of 2026, with 66 GW under construction and 95% of that pipeline pre-committed. CBRE's separate H1 2026 survey reported vacancy of 1.4% across its North American primary markets. These are provider-specific measures with different market coverage and definitions, not directly comparable observations of one identical series. Both point to tight availability. J.P. Morgan Asset Management's November outlook also points to rising GPU rental rates and firm second-hand prices for older Nvidia accelerators. Taken together, the evidence suggests that near-term demand is real. It does not establish that every planned facility will earn an adequate return over its life: pre-commitments are not the same as delivered capacity, utilisation or cash collected. JLL's 11 August release and CBRE's H1 2026 report provide the respective market measures.

The same J.P. Morgan outlook estimates that Nvidia accounts for 28% of announced frontier-lab compute agreements measured in gigawatts for deployment in 2026 or later, with the balance assigned to alternative suppliers and custom chips. These are announced agreements and planned capacity, not delivered systems or realised revenue shares. The figures nevertheless underline the competitive point: customers are distributing future capacity across a broader supplier base, which may influence pricing power and margins over time.

There is also a portfolio-concentration dimension. J.P. Morgan's analysis estimates that Nvidia represented 8.5% of S&P 500 capitalisation by May 2026, compared with roughly 1% in December 2022. An index fund may hold hundreds of companies while carrying a material exposure to one AI supplier and the same underlying investment cycle. Geographic diversification does not necessarily diversify earnings drivers or customer dependencies.

Both conditions can be true: current demand for compute can be exceptionally strong, while the future distribution of returns remains uncertain. Some infrastructure owners may benefit from scarce capacity and rising rents; others may face high power costs, delays, expensive refinancing or insufficient utilisation.

The case for a durable investment cycle

The countercase is substantial. The largest cloud platforms entered this investment phase with established customer bases, significant operating cash flows and access to deep capital markets. Their spending is not solely a speculative bet on a future market: it is also a response to current demand for cloud computing and AI services, and to the strategic risk of being capacity-constrained while competitors expand. Long-lived data-centre assets, power infrastructure and network investments may support several generations of workloads, rather than depending on a single model or chip cycle. Facilities can also be upgraded or repurposed, although doing so may require further capital.

Demand could broaden beyond the largest technology firms as AI tools become embedded in enterprise software, professional services and internal business processes. If that adoption produces recurring customer revenue, higher utilisation and measurable productivity gains, today's infrastructure may be used more intensively over time. Pre-commitments can reduce demand risk for developers when counterparties are creditworthy and contracts allocate power, construction and cancellation risks sensibly.

This case would be strengthened by evidence that customers are renewing and expanding paid deployments, that cash receipts are keeping pace with recognised revenue, and that utilisation and returns improve as capacity comes online. It would be weakened if demand remains concentrated among a small group of heavily investing counterparties, if revenue growth fails to convert into cash, or if new capacity pushes down prices before fixed costs can be recovered. The long-run opportunity can be real even if some projects, suppliers or valuations prove uneconomic.

A two-dimensional stress test for AI investment

Company analysis often treats demand and financing as separate questions. They are better assessed together. The framework below places businesses along two dimensions: the strength of evidence that end users are paying for the service, and the resilience of the business's financing if access to fresh capital becomes more difficult. The axes are intended to be assessed from observable company-level evidence, over time and against the economics of the relevant business model.

Monetised end-user demand should be judged by recurring revenue from independent customers, cash collections relative to recognised revenue, renewal and retention rates, customer concentration, and utilisation or revenue per unit of deployed capacity. Announced contracts, bookings and usage without corresponding payment are weaker evidence. Funding resilience should be judged by operating cash flow after capital expenditure, interest coverage, liquidity, debt maturities and refinancing needs, and the scale and tenor of lease, guarantee and purchase commitments. No single metric is decisive: the point is to test whether cash generation and funding capacity can withstand a slower growth or tighter-credit scenario.

The upper-right quadrant is the most attractive in principle, but it is not a free pass. Businesses with established demand and resilient financing can still be poor investments if valuation already discounts years of exceptional growth, if competition erodes margins or if replacement spending absorbs cash flow. The framework is a way to organise evidence and stress scenarios, not a scorecard with universal thresholds. A business may move between quadrants as cash conversion, utilisation, capital intensity or financing conditions change.

The upper-left quadrant captures businesses with customers and revenues but a financing structure that remains sensitive to refinancing conditions. The lower-left is more exposed: uncertain monetisation coincides with dependence on continued external capital. The lower-right has runway, but still needs to prove that customers will pay enough to cover the full cost of delivery.

Governance overlays every quadrant. A company can have strong demand and a healthy balance sheet while remaining exposed to weak oversight, concentrated decision-making or unclear succession. Equally, a well-governed company can still make poor capital-allocation decisions. These risks should be assessed together, not substituted for one another.

What matters most over the next year

Three indicators deserve priority because they connect demand, returns and the ability to finance the build-out:

  • Cash conversion from end customers: whether recurring revenue and cash collections grow alongside reported usage, bookings and contracted capacity, particularly among independent customers rather than counterparties funded by suppliers.
  • Utilisation and returns on deployed capital: whether operating facilities sustain high utilisation and pricing after power, networking, depreciation and accelerator-replacement costs. Capacity additions alone are not evidence of attractive returns.
  • Funding against fixed obligations: whether operating cash flow can support a rising share of capital expenditure, interest and lease payments, while refinancing needs and contingent commitments remain manageable.

Competition, physical capacity and governance remain important cross-checks. In particular, board independence, compliance controls and credible succession planning matter regardless of where a company sits on the two financial dimensions.

What would change my view?

The constructive near-term demand assessment would strengthen if companies show sustained growth in cash collected from independent customers, rising utilisation and returns on deployed capacity, and capital expenditure increasingly funded from operating cash flow. Stable margins despite new competition would add to that evidence.

I would become more cautious if recognised revenue and announced commitments continue to outpace cash collection; if utilisation, rental rates or pre-leasing weaken as new capacity arrives; or if companies rely more heavily on debt, leases, guarantees and refinancing to maintain investment plans. Delays in power connections or project cancellations would also matter if they reveal that announced demand is not translating into usable capacity.

These are observable tests, not short-term market triggers. The central question is whether end-customer cash flows validate the scale and cost of the infrastructure being built.

The reinstatement resolves continuity, not the investment case

Huang's return removes an immediate leadership vacuum at Nvidia. It does not, by itself, settle questions about board independence, national-security oversight, export controls or the durability of the company's competitive position. Nor does it answer the broader question facing the AI infrastructure cycle: whether the cash generated by end users will ultimately justify the capital committed across the supply chain.

The AI build-out can remain economically transformative while producing uneven returns for investors. Some firms will convert demand into cash and durable pricing power. Others may discover that capacity was built ahead of monetisation, that financing assumptions were too generous, or that the value created accrued elsewhere in the ecosystem.

The useful discipline is to follow the customer's cash, examine the contractual obligations and test whether the institution can withstand disruption. Jensen Huang's reinstatement makes that last test especially visible. A valuable founder can accelerate a company; a resilient institution must be able to outlast any one individual.
