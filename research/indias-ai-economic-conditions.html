Measuring India's AI Economic Conditions: From Adoption to Productivity

By Shiny Kundu · 22 November 2026 · Research framework and baseline

Bottom line. India is a significant user of generative AI platforms, but observed platform traffic is not a measure of economy-wide adoption. Anthropic's India country brief finds that India accounts for 5.8% of global Claude.ai use, yet ranks 101st of 116 countries on a working-age-population-adjusted measure. Separate survey evidence points to substantial self-reported use among working-age adults and enterprise respondents, but the sources cover different populations and have distinct sampling limitations. This article sets out an independent India AI Economic Conditions Index (IAECI) framework and a baseline evidence dashboard. The broader index remains a proposed measurement architecture, not a measured quarterly series.

Why measure economic conditions rather than AI adoption alone?

AI adoption surveys often count firms experimenting with a tool, employees using a chatbot or executives expecting productivity gains. These are useful indicators of diffusion, but they do not establish that output per worker has risen, that firms have redesigned production, or that workers have moved into more productive employment. Adoption is an input into a production process. Its economic return depends on complementary investment in data, software, organisational capital, skills, management and infrastructure.

The distinction is central to the recent work of Anthropic's economists Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory. Their March 2026 paper, “Labor market impacts of AI: A new measure and early evidence”, combines theoretical task capability with observed Claude use, giving greater weight to work-related automation than augmentation. The authors find no systematic increase in unemployment among highly exposed US workers since late 2022, while reporting suggestive evidence of slower hiring among younger workers in exposed occupations. They emphasise the limits of exposure measures and the difficulty of identifying causal effects in labour-market data. The framework is valuable precisely because it separates technical feasibility from observed use and realised employment outcomes.

The June 2026 Anthropic Economic Index report, “Cadences”, extends the measurement problem. As Claude Code and Cowork generate longer-running, more agentic workflows, conversation transcripts alone become less informative. The report adds higher-frequency sampling, output classification and monthly aggregates across Claude conversations and first-party API traffic. It also links usage patterns to survey evidence on workers' expectations. The January 2026 report on economic primitives adds task complexity, human and AI skills, use case, autonomy and task success to the earlier automation-augmentation distinction.

These developments suggest a broader measurement framework for India. It should capture the breadth of adoption, the economic quality of use and the labour-market adjustment that follows. It should also distinguish the observed behaviour of users of one AI platform from the adoption of AI across the Indian economy.

India's starting position: scale without broad diffusion

Anthropic's India Country Brief: The Anthropic Economic Index, published on 16 February 2026, analyses approximately 58,000 Claude.ai conversations from India in a global sample of 975,160 conversations collected between 13 and 20 November 2025. India accounted for 5.8% of observed global Claude.ai use, the second-largest national share. Yet it ranked 101st among 116 countries with sufficient observations when usage was adjusted for the working-age population. The contrast is important: India's absolute contribution to platform traffic is large, while average observed use per working-age person remains comparatively low.

Adoption is also spatially concentrated. Maharashtra accounted for 15.5% of India's observed Claude.ai use, Tamil Nadu 13.2%, Karnataka 12.7% and Delhi 10.5%. Together, these four jurisdictions represented 51.9% of the India sample. This is consistent with a concentration of AI use in established technology, services and metropolitan labour markets. It is not a direct measure of state-level AI adoption rates: the denominator is observed Claude use, not each state's workforce or business population.

The occupational composition is similarly concentrated. Software-related tasks represented 45.2% of India's O*NET-mapped Claude use, the highest share among the countries compared in the brief. This points to an important comparative advantage in software and technical services, but also to a measurement risk. Platform data may over-represent workers and firms already comfortable with digital tools. The result should not be extrapolated to manufacturing, agriculture, informal services or the full distribution of Indian enterprises.

Anthropic estimates that tasks in the India sample would take an average of 3.8 hours without AI and 14.8 minutes with it, a ratio of approximately 15.4 to one. The global comparison is 3.1 hours and 15.4 minutes, or about 12.1 to one. These are task-level time estimates inferred from conversations, not measured changes in firm output, worker hours, wages or total factor productivity. They should be read as evidence of perceived or estimated task compression among observed users, not as a 15-fold increase in economy-wide productivity.

Work-related use accounted for 51.3% of the India sample, compared with 46.0% globally. The autonomy score was 3.60 against 3.38 globally on a five-point scale. The share of tasks judged completable by a human alone was lower in India, at 84.6% against 87.9%. Taken together, these measures suggest that Indian users in the sample are bringing relatively demanding tasks to Claude and delegating a greater degree of decision-making. They do not establish how often these workflows are adopted by employers or how much value is captured by workers and firms.

The proposed India AI Economic Conditions Index

The IAECI is designed as a quarterly, three-pillar composite. It adapts the task-based logic of Massenkoff and McCrory to Indian occupational classifications and adds firm-level diffusion and realised labour-market outcomes. The unit of analysis should be the Indian economy, with state, industry, firm-size and occupation breakdowns wherever the data permit.

PillarWhat it measuresCandidate indicatorsEconomic interpretation
1. Diffusion and accessHow widely AI reaches workers, firms and regions.Per-capita AI use; firm adoption by size and sector; production deployment rather than pilots; geographic and occupational concentration; access to compute, connectivity and relevant skills.Whether AI is spreading beyond early-adopter firms and metropolitan technology clusters.
2. Productive integrationWhether AI is embedded in economically meaningful workflows.Work-related use; automation versus augmentation; task complexity and success; autonomy; time saved; process redesign; firm-reported productivity and realised return on investment.Whether use changes production processes and raises the capacity to produce goods and services.
3. Labour-market transmissionHow exposure and adoption affect the quantity and quality of work.Employment and hiring in exposed occupations; entry-level job starts; hours and earnings; transitions between occupations; vacancies, separations and unemployment; training and task reallocation.Whether productivity gains complement workers, alter labour demand or create adjustment costs.

The proposed composite is:

IAECIt = (Dt + Pt + Lt) / 3

where D, P and L are the diffusion, productive-integration and labour-market-transmission sub-indices. Each indicator would first be oriented so that a higher value consistently represents stronger economic conditions, then standardised against a fixed reference distribution and aggregated within its pillar. Equal pillar weights are proposed for the initial specification to avoid an appearance of precision unsupported by evidence. Alternative weights and aggregation methods would be reported as sensitivity tests. Concentration measures would enter with a negative sign in the diffusion pillar; employment and wage outcomes would be assessed relative to suitable comparison groups rather than treated as automatically positive or negative.

The five-indicator baseline is a descriptive evidence dashboard, not a composite. Three indicators come from the Rutgers tracker and are related: “any use” includes work use, while the time-savings estimate is calculated from the same survey system. Giving these measures separate equal weights would count overlapping evidence more than once. The Anthropic country ranking is ordinal and platform-specific; converting it into a 0–100 cardinal score would impose an unvalidated scale. The Rutgers time-savings figure likewise cannot be normalised against an arbitrary 10% ceiling and interpreted as productivity. Combining these observations with Deloitte's enterprise sample would further mix distinct populations and reference periods. For these reasons, no composite is calculated or reported in this edition. The component observations remain available in the CSV, with their source limitations stated.

What the latest India evidence adds

The Rutgers GenAI Adoption Tracker India reports that 68.1% of working-age adults in its pooled April 2026 estimate used generative AI for any purpose, while 35.7% used it at work. It estimates time savings equivalent to 3.16% of total work hours. The pooled sample comprises 3,256 respondents across two online waves and a one-time rural Uttar Pradesh face-to-face supplement: online wave 1 (N=1,765), rural supplement (N=508) and online wave 2 (N=983). Estimates are weighted by age, gender and urbanicity for adoption measures, and by age and gender for work-hours savings. The tracker explicitly cautions that online recruitment and self-selection may make adoption estimates upper bounds. The rural supplement improves coverage of populations less likely to enter an online panel, but it is not a recurring tracker wave.

Enterprise survey evidence measures a different population. Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise reports that 40% of its Indian respondents describe AI use as significant or full. The global survey covered 3,235 senior leaders between August and September 2025, including 200 respondents in India; respondents were split equally between IT and line-of-business leaders. The participating organisations already had at least one working AI implementation in daily use, so the sample is drawn from AI-active organisations and cannot represent all Indian firms. Deloitte's public methodology does not provide the India subgroup's weighting details or the exact wording and response categories behind the 40% figure. It is therefore cited here as a reported survey result, not a national enterprise-adoption rate. Deloitte also reports at-scale deployment across several functions, but those results share the same selected enterprise sample.

The 3.16% work-hours-saving estimate is a self-reported time-saving measure. It is not equivalent to a 3.16% increase in labour productivity. Time saved may be spent checking outputs, correcting errors, undertaking additional tasks or absorbed by organisational slack. Productivity requires output relative to hours and other inputs; it must be measured at the firm or sector level before an aggregate effect can be inferred.

Building an India-specific data architecture

Three data systems can anchor the next stage of the index.

First, Anthropic's open Economic Index data. The public dataset includes successive releases, task mappings and the labour-market exposure files. The March 2026 paper's observed-exposure approach combines theoretical LLM capability, real-world task use, work-related context and the relative weight of automation versus augmentation. Its US occupational results cannot simply be transferred to India. An Indian implementation would require a documented crosswalk from O*NET tasks and SOC occupations to India's National Classification of Occupations, with task weights and mapping uncertainty made explicit.

Second, firm-level adoption and productivity. The World Bank's India 2026 Enterprise Survey follow-up on Artificial Intelligence is especially promising. It covers a nationally representative sample of registered establishments with at least five employees, with 1,355 follow-up interviews conducted in March and April 2026. The questionnaire covers AI use, business operations, jobs, constraints, expectations and labour utilisation, and the follow-up can be linked to the 2025–26 baseline Enterprise Survey. This is a stronger basis for measuring firm adoption and adjustment than voluntary executive polls. The survey's coverage excludes smaller and unregistered enterprises, so the informal and micro-enterprise economy will remain an important blind spot.

Third, worker outcomes. The Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation's PLFS provides employment, unemployment, labour-force participation, occupation and earnings information. The 2025 annual report records a usual-status labour-force participation rate of 59.3% for people aged 15 and above, broadly stable from 2024, and an increase in the share of regular wage or salaried workers to 23.6% from 22.4%. The revamped survey design and occupation coding need to be handled carefully when constructing a time series. Linking PLFS microdata to an India-specific task-exposure measure would allow the index to monitor employment, hiring and earnings across more- and less-exposed occupations.

These sources are complementary, not interchangeable. Anthropic data reveal platform-level task use; enterprise surveys reveal reported firm adoption and adjustment; PLFS records worker outcomes. Their populations, frequencies, units of observation and sampling frames differ. The index should retain source-specific sub-indicators and publish a data-vintage table rather than merge incompatible series into a single growth rate.

What the index can and cannot say about productivity

The productivity channel is not automatic. A worker may complete a task more quickly without increasing measured output if saved time is absorbed by review, coordination or idle capacity. A firm may report adoption without changing its production function. A business may raise output per employee while reducing employment, or use productivity gains to expand output and hiring. At the aggregate level, AI can raise measured productivity only if task-level gains survive implementation costs, diffuse across firms and sectors, and appear in value added relative to labour and capital inputs.

This is why the IAECI places productive integration between adoption and labour-market outcomes. The relevant empirical question is not simply whether firms use AI, but whether adoption is associated with measurable changes in output, value added, costs, quality, product innovation and worker time allocation. A firm-level panel would help distinguish selection from impact: more productive firms may be more likely to adopt AI in the first place. Credible causal estimates would require a comparison strategy that addresses this selection, alongside industry, firm size, capital intensity, demand and management quality.

The labour-market pillar should likewise avoid treating occupational exposure as displacement. Massenkoff and McCrory's US analysis finds no systematic increase in unemployment among highly exposed workers, but identifies suggestive evidence of slower hiring among younger workers in exposed occupations. For India, the relevant tests include entry-level recruitment, formal and informal employment transitions, real earnings, hours, job quality and movement into complementary tasks. India's large services-export sector and its diverse informal workforce make the adjustment process likely to differ from the US experience.

Initial reading: a strong frontier, a narrow base

The first baseline points to a dual picture. India's observed Claude use is substantial in absolute terms and appears relatively sophisticated in its work orientation, autonomy and task complexity. The time-compression estimates are striking, although they remain task-level measures rather than realised productivity statistics. At the same time, per-capita use is low relative to the global distribution, software tasks account for a large share of observed activity, and usage is concentrated across a handful of states.

The near-term economic opportunity is to broaden productive use beyond the existing technology and professional-services base. That requires firm-level integration, reliable data infrastructure, worker training, managerial capability and access for smaller enterprises. The risk is a two-speed transition in which productivity gains accrue to already productive firms and skilled workers while adoption costs, weak complementary skills and limited access slow diffusion elsewhere.

The index will be useful only if it can distinguish those outcomes over time. The next releases should track whether adoption spreads across sectors and firm sizes, whether task-level time savings translate into firm-level productivity, and whether hiring and earnings in exposed occupations adjust differently from comparable work. These are measurable questions. A composite should be considered only after the indicators have a coherent measurement model, defensible reference bounds, sensitivity analysis and a sufficiently consistent time series.

Conclusion

India's AI transition should be evaluated as a process of economic diffusion and reorganisation, not a count of users or pilots. The Anthropic Economic Index provides a valuable new lens on observed task use. The work of Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory adds a disciplined distinction between technical capability, observed exposure and labour-market outcomes. India's own enterprise and labour-force data can extend that framework to the structure of its economy.

The proposed India AI Economic Conditions Index brings these strands together in a transparent measurement system. The current baseline is a set of source-specific indicators, not a definitive national score. The central question for policymakers and investors is whether AI becomes a broad-based source of productivity and better work, or remains concentrated in a narrow segment of firms, occupations and regions. The answer will be found in firm-level performance and worker outcomes, not platform traffic alone.
